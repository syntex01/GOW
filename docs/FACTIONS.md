# The Five Creeds

What each one is, what it looks like, how it plays, what it builds, what it does
to you, and how it dies.

Read `ECONOMY.md` first — the four bottlenecks (income, research, production,
defence) and the two unit curves (line units flat-cost and linear, elites
exponential in both) are the frame everything below hangs on. A creed does not
get more of everything. It **bends one bottleneck past where anyone else can
reach it, and pays for that somewhere it cannot hide.**

Each creed has three or four **paths**. Paths are not classes — they are
directions, and the interesting builds are the hybrids. Every section below says
what happens when you take two.

---

## CARNAGE — the war feeds on what it kills

> *Everyone else fights over the field. Carnage fights over what is lying on it.*

**Bends: production.** The cheapest soldiers in the game, and its dead are not a
loss — they are inventory.

### How it looks

Bone white and arterial red. Hooks, chains, banners of stretched skin. Carnage
soldiers are ragged and asymmetric — no two silhouettes quite match, because
nothing here was issued, it was taken. They move in a loose crowd rather than a
formation, and there are always more of them than you can count at a glance.

Its buildings are **wet**. The Bone Kiln is a brick cone with a stoke hole
glowing at the base and a stack of material against one side that visibly grows
as the harvest comes in. The Ossuary is a low vault with ribs for buttresses.
When a Carnage building is destroyed it does not explode — it *slumps*.

The signature effect is the ground. Carnage half-fields go dark and stay dark:
blood that does not wash off, mounds of settled dead that rise out of the road
and physically change its shape. You can tell how long a fight has been running
in one place by how high the ground is.

### The paths

**THE TIDE — volume, and volume that gets angrier**

Line units cost **no population at all** and build in half the time. Every corpse
on your half of the field adds a little damage to every one of your line units.

You do not win the first engagement at a choke point. You lose it. You lose the
second one too. You win the fourth, standing on the first three, because by then
your soldiers hit forty per cent harder than they did and theirs do not.

*The scary part:* the enemy's own kills are what is arming you. A player fighting
the Tide has to make a real decision about whether to keep trading — and the
alternative to trading is giving up ground.

- **Charnel Yard** — line units population-free, built at double speed. This is
  the building that makes the whole path exist.
- **Bone Kiln** *(exists)* — remains pay 0.9 gold/s each instead of 0.45. Burn it
  and they pay nothing for thirty seconds, which is the correct counter and should
  hurt.
- Research: **Butchery** already turns every death into a dismemberment. Extend it
  so dismembered bodies count double toward the corpse bonus — a Carnage player
  who takes Butchery is choosing to make the field *messier on purpose*.

**THE RISEN — recursion**

Your dead get back up. Not once, as a trick — continuously, as an economy.

- **Ossuary** — **banks** corpses instead of consuming what happens to be lying
  nearby. This is the key change: it lets a Carnage commander *save deaths up*
  through a quiet minute and cash the whole bank in during one push. A full
  Ossuary emptying itself is thirty soldiers standing up at once.
- **Resurrection Vat** *(exists)* — the dead rise every 2.4s instead of 4, at 65%
  health instead of 50%.
- Corpse mounds should **absorb ranged damage** in their own file. A Carnage line
  that has held one spot for two minutes is fighting from behind a wall it built
  out of its own casualties, and the only way through is to physically clear it.

**THE BUTCHER — one enormous answer**

Carnage's problem is that a hundred cheap soldiers lose to one thing that is
immune to being swarmed. The Butcher is Carnage solving that itself.

A single champion. Lifesteal. It grows **permanently** with every kill — and it
grows *visibly*: the silhouette gets bigger, the weapon gets bigger, at fifty
kills it is taller than the soldiers around it, at a hundred it is a landmark.

Expensive, slow, and desperately vulnerable for the first ninety seconds. A
Butcher that dies at ten kills is a catastrophe you may not recover from. One that
reaches a hundred cannot be handled by anything that arrives in twos.

**THE HUNGER — losing is fuel** *(new)*

The dark option. Your army gets faster and angrier as your **own** population
drops. At full strength you are sluggish. At a quarter strength every soldier you
have left moves and swings at nearly double rate.

This makes Carnage terrifying to finish off. An opponent who has broken your line
and is walking in to end it is walking into the strongest version of you. It also
makes Carnage genuinely bad at defending a lead — you are weakest when you are
winning, which is a real, legible cost.

### Paths together

- **Tide + Risen** is the classic grinder and the corpses do double duty: they buff
  the living *and* fill the Ossuary. It is slow, inevitable, and almost impossible
  to push into once it has a mound.
- **Tide + Butcher** is the strongest all-round Carnage: the chaff exists to feed
  the champion kills. Every soldier you spend is a percentage on the Butcher.
- **Hunger + Risen** is the panic build — you get stronger as you die and then the
  dead come back angry. Its weakness is that it needs to actually be losing, which
  is a bad plan against anyone who kills you quickly.
- **Butcher + Hunger** is a coin flip you can build on purpose: one champion,
  minimal support, and the fewer soldiers you have the faster it swings.

### Which core buildings it wants

**Muster Yard first, and to the top.** Carnage cares about production above
everything — build slots and population are its whole game. Granary second.
Reliquary is nearly a dump stat: Carnage's power comes from bodies, not from
technology. It skips defence entirely, which is exactly why fire hurts.

### How it loses

Fire and area damage clear the tide **and burn the fuel it runs on** at the same
time — Ordnance is its natural predator and the matchup is not close if the Cinder
Host gets its batteries up. And a Tide with no Butcher has no answer at all to one
enormous thing walking down the middle.

## ORDNANCE — the ground is the weapon

> *The infantry are not very good. They are not supposed to be. They are there to
> be in the way while the guns work.*

**Bends: defence, outward.** Ordnance is the only creed that projects its defence
onto ground it does not hold.

### How it looks

Soot black, ember orange, brass fittings, and smoke that never quite clears. Its
soldiers are uniform in a way Carnage's are not — issued kit, matching coats,
identical rifles — which reads as *cheap and numerous* rather than elite.

Its buildings are heavy and half-buried. The Powder Magazine is a bermed bunker
with a hazard-striped blast door and a vent stack, barrels stacked outside that
nobody has moved. Batteries are squat, wide, and recoil visibly when they fire.

The signature effect is the **sky**. When a Cinder Host player has a battery line
up, the air over the enemy half is never empty: shells arcing over, flashes on the
horizon, ash drifting down across the whole board. You do not see the guns. You see
what they are doing, everywhere, all the time.

### The paths

**THE BARRAGE — artillery stops being units**

Artillery becomes **buildings**. Batteries sit on rear plots and shell the field on
their own, forever, without population, without being walked anywhere and without
being killed by the thing they are shooting at.

More batteries, more shells. Your infantry exists to stop anything walking up to
the guns.

*The scary part:* a completed battery line means the entire midfield is
permanently under fire. Crossing it costs you soldiers whether or not there is
anything there to fight. Ordnance is the only creed that makes **empty ground**
expensive.

- **Battery** — rear-plot building that fires on the field. Tiers add range, then
  rate, then a second barrel. Three batteries is a nuisance; six is a wall of fire.
- **Signal Tower** *(exists)* — plunging fire reaches any file. With batteries this
  is what turns a line of guns into a weapon that covers the whole board rather
  than one lane of it.
- **Powder Magazine** *(exists)* — +18% splash on everything you fire, and it
  detonates in your own yard when razed.

**THE FIRESTORM — make the ground unusable**

You do not kill the army. You make the place it has to walk through uninhabitable,
and then kill whatever staggers out.

- Fire zones that persist and spread. Corpses burn where they fall.
- Research: extend **Ashfall** so burning ground slows and disorients. An army that
  crosses a firestorm arrives *disorganised* — and disorganised is the state in
  which cheap infantry can finish something expensive.

**THE LINE — massed volley**

Damage scales with **how many of your riflemen are in the same file**. Ten in one
lane hit far harder than ten spread across five.

This is the one creed for which stacking a single lane is correct, and it makes
Ordnance a spam faction that plays nothing like Carnage: Carnage wants to be
everywhere, Ordnance wants to be *concentrated*. Visually it is a rank of rifles
going off as one sound and one flash.

**THE FORWARD MAGAZINE — a bomb you leave for them** *(new)*

The joke that is also a strategy. Build a Powder Magazine on a **forward** plot
deliberately, where the enemy will reach it. It detonates when destroyed. You are
not defending it. You are leaving it where their army will be standing.

Deeply Cinder Host, genuinely strong against anything that pushes in a mass, and
completely useless against a careful opponent who shoots it from range.

### Paths together

- **Barrage + Firestorm** is the control build and the most oppressive thing in the
  game to play against: the ground is on fire and the fire is being resupplied from
  buildings you cannot reach.
- **Barrage + Line** concentrates everything — one file, guns behind it, and
  anything that comes down that lane dies. Weak to being flanked in the four files
  you left empty.
- **Firestorm + Forward Magazine** turns your own half into a minefield you are
  happy to fight in.

### Which core buildings it wants

**Granary and Forge.** Batteries are buildings, so Ordnance is competing with
itself for plots — it needs income to afford them and structure health to keep
them. It uses Muster Yard less than Carnage (its infantry is a screen, not a win
condition) and Reliquary about averagely.

### How it loses

Anything that closes the distance fast. Its infantry cannot hold a line against
elites, and once something is standing on the batteries the whole build is over.
Blight taking the ground its guns need to see blinds it completely.

## ENGINEERING — few, enormous, and permanently improving

> *It does not get stronger when it fights. It gets stronger when it survives.*

**Bends: research.** The only creed whose power keeps climbing while it does
nothing at all.

### How it looks

Cold steel blue, exposed cabling, arc light. Everything modular, bolted, and
obviously *manufactured* — the visual opposite of Carnage. Its units are few and
large and they walk with weight.

Its buildings are the cleanest in the game: the Assembly Line is a sawtooth
factory roof with north-light glazing, a conveyor running out of one end and a
tall extraction stack. Nothing about an Engineering yard is improvised.

The signature effect is **accumulation you can see**. Research Halls glow brighter
the longer they stand. An opponent looking across the field can read exactly how
long an Engineering player has been left alone, and exactly how much trouble that
means.

### The paths

**THE ASCENDANT — permanent, unbounded, and only while it stands**

Rush ascension. After ascending, every Research Hall on your ground grants a
small **permanent** buff every twenty seconds. Not a percentage that caps — a
counter that keeps going up for as long as the building is standing.

This does three things at once, which is why it is the best idea in this
document. It makes a turtle that is genuinely more dangerous the longer it lives.
It gives the opponent an urgent and *specific* target — those buildings, right
there, and the clock is running. And it makes forward plots worth the risk,
because a Research Hall on a **forward** plot produces double.

The greedy play is to put your most precious building in the most exposed place on
the board. That is a real decision and it is visible to both players.

*The scary part:* an Engineering player at minute fifteen with four halls standing
has units that are simply better than anything else in the game, and the number is
still going up. There is no soft cap to wait out. You either killed the halls or
you lost.

- **Research Hall** — the permanent-buff building. Double output on a forward plot.
- **Assembly Line** *(exists)* — turret wrecks reprint instantly and at full
  strength.
- Research: an ascension node that converts Halls from "faster research" into
  "permanent army buffs", so ascension is a change of *state* rather than a bigger
  number.

**THE FOUNDRY — one colossus, forever**

You never field more than about three units. Each one is worth forty. When one
dies the Assembly Line builds it again **for free**.

Visually a Foundry unit fills the lane vertically — it is taller than the fortress
plots around it and it does not flinch.

The tension is that three units cannot cover five files, so the Foundry player is
always making a choice about which lane to concede.

**THE LATTICE — defences that hold each other up** *(new)*

Turrets, walls and defensive buildings **link**. Adjacent defences share health:
damage to one is spread across the network, so the whole line has to be broken at
once rather than picked apart one emplacement at a time.

A completed Lattice is the hardest thing in the game to crack from the front, and
it is entirely useless against anything that goes around it — which is why the
Lattice player also needs the Ascendant's forward halls to be worth playing.

**THE FORWARD DOCTRINE — research under fire** *(new)*

The aggressive research build. Everything valuable goes on forward plots for
double output, protected by a Lattice, deliberately inside the enemy's reach. You
are not turtling — you are camping on the midfield and daring them to come.

### Paths together

- **Ascendant + Lattice** is the intended shape: unbounded growth behind a wall
  that has to be broken all at once. Beat the clock or lose.
- **Ascendant + Forward Doctrine** doubles the growth rate and halves the safety.
  The highest-ceiling, highest-risk build in the game.
- **Foundry + Lattice** is the fortress build — three colossi and an unbreakable
  front, conceding two files permanently and daring you to exploit it.

### Which core buildings it wants

**Reliquary above all, then Forge.** Engineering is the only creed that genuinely
needs the research bottleneck opened wide, and the only one that needs its
buildings to *survive*, so structure health matters more to it than to anyone.
Muster Yard is almost irrelevant — it is not fielding numbers.

### How it loses

Being rushed before it ascends, and being drowned afterwards. Six units cannot
hold five files: the moment Carnage gets past the wall in one place the whole
thing folds, and Engineering has no way to rebuild an army quickly because it
never had one.

## THE OCCULT — pay in lives, not gold

> *Its best units are not bought. They are summoned, and the enemy watches it
> happen.*

**Bends: the cost model itself.**

### How it looks

Violet and bone. Floating stone, open eyes, things that do not quite touch the
ground. Occult casters are thin and tall and move too smoothly; its thralls are
whatever it turned last, so a Coven army is visually a *mixture* — enemy soldiers
walking backwards, in your colours.

Its buildings are the most dramatic on the board. The Black Chapel is a stepped
ziggurat with a lit arch cut up through the bottom steps and an open, unblinking
eye above it. The Thrall Pit is a hole with a grate bolted over it and something
moving under the bars.

The signature effect is the **glow of a rite in progress**. It is bright, it is
obvious, it pulses faster as it nears completion, and it is visible from across
the field.

### The paths

**THE RITE — a public commitment**

A Summoning Circle is a building that must **survive a fixed time**, and then
consumes itself to put something terrible on the field.

It glows while it works. The enemy can see it, knows exactly what it means, and
has precisely that long to come and kill it.

This is the most interesting building in the game because it is not a secret. You
are announcing that in ninety seconds something is coming, and betting your
position that you can hold the ground until then. Both players are now on the same
clock and both know it.

**THE LONG RITE — a doom clock** *(new)*

The escalation, and the scariest thing in this document. A rite that takes **five
minutes** and summons something that ends the game.

From the moment it starts, both players can see it. The Occult player has
committed everything to holding one building for five minutes. The opponent has
five minutes to get to it, and knows that failing means losing outright.

It converts a whole match into a single legible question with a visible timer.
Used once, it is unforgettable. It should be extremely expensive and there should
be exactly one of them.

**THE COVEN — few casters, screened by the dead**

A handful of extremely strong casters behind a wall of thralls. Area damage that
punishes precisely what Carnage and Ordnance do — which makes the Occult the
anti-spam creed **without owning a single cheap unit of its own**.

- **Thrall Pit** *(exists)* — turned dead cost no population. This is the whole
  screen: chaff you did not pay for, protecting casters you paid a fortune for.

**THE TITHE — win with the ability, not the army**

**Black Chapel** *(exists)* takes 8% of every death anywhere on the field instead
of 5%. Pushed further, an Occult commander who leans all the way in wins with
**abilities** rather than soldiers: the war is a battery and the ability is the
weapon. Every death anywhere — including your own, including ones you had nothing
to do with — is charge.

### Paths together

- **Rite + Coven** is the standard: casters hold the ground while the circle
  works, thralls absorb the push.
- **Tithe + Coven** is the attrition build. You do not need to win fights; you need
  deaths to happen. Feeding your own thralls into a grinder is *correct*.
- **Long Rite + Tithe** is the doomsday build — the deaths from five minutes of
  desperate defence charge an ability you use the moment the summon lands.
- **Long Rite + Lattice-style defence** is not available to the Occult, which is
  the point: it has to hold that ground with bodies and magic, not walls.

### Which core buildings it wants

**Reliquary and Granary.** Rites and casters are expensive and gated behind deep
research. It leans on Muster Yard least of anyone — the Thrall Pit *is* its
production, paid for with other people's soldiers.

### How it loses

A fast push onto a circle mid-rite, which destroys the investment and the tempo in
one stroke. And being simply out-produced while paying elite prices for
everything.

## BLIGHT — take the ground and the army follows

> *It does not want trades. It wants time.*

**Bends: income and defence, indirectly.** Blight is the only creed whose economy
and whose defence are the same object.

### How it looks

Pale green, wet, fungal, pulsing. Blight units are small and numerous and *soft* —
rounded silhouettes, no hard edges, no metal. They do not march so much as spread.

Its buildings grow rather than being built. The Spore Bed is a mycelial mound with
fruiting caps at varied heights. The Heart Root is not architecture at all: a
tapering, leaning, knotted bole with buttress roots hugging the ground and boughs
reaching out of the crown, with the heart showing through a split about a third of
the way up.

The signature effect is the **ground changing colour**. Blight territory creeps
outward visibly, and everything standing on it that is not Blight is slowed,
damaged and unhappy.

### The paths

**THE BLOOM — the map is the win condition**

Spore Beds spread hostile ground on their own, with no kills needed to seed it.
Ground you hold hurts everything that is not yours.

Blight is the only creed that can be **winning while doing nothing**, and the only
one where an opponent has to attack into a disadvantage that is growing whether or
not anyone fights.

**THE AMALGAM — get stronger by not dying**

Small growths that touch each other **merge** into a bigger one. Three become one.
Three of those become one again.

This inverts every other creed's relationship with combat. Everyone else trades.
Blight *accumulates*. A Blight army that has been left alone for four minutes is
not a bigger crowd — it is a smaller number of much larger things, and the
transition happens visibly, mid-fight, as thirty small units become three enormous
ones in front of you.

- **Spawning Pool** — turns out cheap growths continuously, a slow autospawn that
  feeds the merge.

**THE SPREAD — a creep that reaches the fortress** *(new)*

Blight grows toward the enemy base on its own. If it arrives, it begins damaging
the fortress directly.

A slow, inevitable, entirely visible advance that the opponent must go and cut
back — which means leaving their line. It is the least violent win condition in
the game and by far the most frustrating to be on the wrong end of.

**THE ROOT — impossible to push into**

**Heart Root** *(exists)* heals your buildings and mires everything else. Commit
here and you become something that cannot be entered: the ground fights, buildings
repair faster than they can be burned, and every step onto your half is slower
than the last.

### Paths together

- **Bloom + Spread** is the map-control build: your ground grows, and then it grows
  *at them*. You may never attack at all.
- **Bloom + Amalgam** is the standard: your territory keeps your growths alive long
  enough to merge.
- **Root + Spread** is the siege build — unpushable, and slowly arriving.
- **Amalgam + Root** is the brawl build: enormous merged things standing in healing
  ground.

### Which core buildings it wants

**Granary and Muster Yard**, but unusually it wants them at *low tiers across many
plots* rather than one at maximum — Blight is a wide creed, not a tall one. Forge
matters because the Heart Root synergises with structure repair.

### How it loses

Fire, which burns blight faster than it grows — the Cinder Host again. And ranged
armies that simply never walk into it and shell the beds from outside, which is
why Blight's worst matchup is a Barrage that refuses to advance.

## The counter-web

| beats | is beaten by | because |
|---|---|---|
| Ordnance → Carnage | | fire clears the tide and burns the corpses that fuel it |
| Carnage → Engineering | | six units cannot hold five files |
| Engineering → Occult | | permanent buffs outlast a timed rite |
| Occult → Ordnance | | area casters punish massed cheap infantry |
| Blight → anything slow | fire, and ranged armies that never enter | |

Nobody lacks an answer. Every answer costs you the thing you were otherwise
buying.

## What makes the late game tense

The current late game is quiet: a small army, a full bank, and progression that
already happened. Five things fix that, and they are all consequences of the
economy document rather than separate features.

1. **The field is full.** Line units at flat cost and Muster-driven population mean
   late fights are fifty a side and more, and the field *refills* — losing an
   engagement is not the end of the game, it is the start of the next one.
2. **There are focal points.** Elites are rare, enormous and expensive, so a late
   battle has three or four things in it that everybody on the board is reacting
   to. A Butcher at eighty kills, a colossus, a merged Amalgam — the fight has a
   shape.
3. **The ground tells the story.** Corpse mounds rise, craters accumulate, blight
   spreads, fire lingers. By minute twenty the middle of the map is a legible
   record of the whole match, and it affects how the next fight goes.
4. **Everyone has a visible weakness.** The four bottlenecks mean a specialised
   opponent is *readably* deficient somewhere, and you can see which. The late game
   becomes a question of whether you can reach the thing they neglected before they
   reach the thing you did.
5. **There are clocks both players can see.** A rite counting down, Research Halls
   glowing brighter, blight creeping toward a fortress, an Ossuary filling. The
   most tense moments in a strategy game are the ones where both players know
   exactly what is coming and exactly how long they have.
